2. DIM IS LESS PRESENCE — and the opacity floor is MEASURED, not chosen.
   Same fill, same ink. What goes is opacity, elevation and shadow.
   On a light ground, fading a DARK token pulls it toward the paper and costs
   contrast directly, so there is a hard floor. Searched across all four worlds
   (tools/dim-floor.mts):
     alpha 1.00  5.14:1     0.90  4.35:1     0.80  3.68:1
     alpha 0.78  3.55:1  <- shipped
     alpha 0.70  3.08:1  <- lowest that still clears 3:1
     alpha 0.68  2.97:1  <- below
   Most of the dimming therefore has to come from elevation and shadow, which
   cost no contrast at all. A tile lying flat casting nothing is plainly not
   pickable however close its colour still is — which is why 9.6's formulation
   works here at all.

3. MATERIAL
   Inner shadow along the top edge, rim light along the bottom, shared grain.
   Deliberately the INVERSE of the usual raised-button bevel: that lighting says
   "a control protruding from a page", this says "a solid thing sitting in the
   light of the room". With the drop shadow underneath it, a tile reads as an
   object resting ON the paper rather than drawn on it.
   Grain: ONE 64x64 tile, generated (tools/make-grain.mjs), used by every token
   type and by the tray. Per-pixel noise tiles seamlessly by construction — no
   seam work — which is why grain rather than a fibre or weave.

4. FURNITURE
   Lane: squared paper. The white veil still does the separation job (9.1); the
   ruling is drawn over it at a fixed 22px pitch, so the squares stay square
   whatever height the lane takes for a given board.
   Pool: a shallow wooden tray, translucent at 0.55. Translucency is not a
   styling choice — an opaque tray would become the real surface behind every
   tile, and the gate would be measuring paper the player never sees. It is also
   contrast-POSITIVE: the wood is lighter than the darkest paper, so it lifts
   the worst points rather than deepening them.
